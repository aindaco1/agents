# Where the paragraph break belongs

Alonso preferred the edit of *The Dam Will Break* that kept more short follow-ups beside their setups. In the original there were 109 body paragraphs. The preferred edit had 76, and the other had 86. Both edits kept the short sentences in the exchange about knowing what the filmmakers were doing. One used an extra break before the dry correction. The reader's preference supplies a reason to try removing that break in an essay. The other two drafts in the blind comparison tied.

## What the magazine pages show

We examined archived pages for 32 pieces under ten bylines. We treated this professionally edited work as human-written for the study. Their publication dates span 2001–2011 and the inspected captures date from 2011–2019. The evidence describes the arrangement on those pages. It cannot distinguish the writer's manuscript decisions from an editor's or the publishing system's. The review writers still used short sentences. Four verified examples end a longer paragraph in eight words or fewer. One critic follows a description of an affected visual style with a four-word verdict. Elsewhere a critic builds an anecdote until a father's exclamation can end it. Both effects depend on material immediately above the short line. Adding a break before every verdict or punchline would remove a timing choice these writers used. The interview totals mean something different. A moment of surprise, a two-word answer or a clarification from the other guest can change whose voice is on the page. Even within one form, the three interviews had 25 of 55, 19 of 57, and 7 of 56 dialogue paragraphs at 12 words or fewer. A personal essay can switch into dialogue for the same reason. In Williams's Santa essay, a short question belongs to the mother and the reply belongs to her daughter. Joining those turns risks changing who seems to be speaking. ## Three further editing decisions

1. **Let one word carry a transition when its meaning changes.** A remark about tourists near the end of *Kate & Leopold* gives Zacharek a way to describe which performer seems out of place. The repeated word connects a small piece of dialogue to a judgment about acting. In Kamiya's essay about his children, the idea of bottling time becomes an image he can use to toast his daughter. A synonym pass could weaken either connection. Before replacing a repeated word, identify what it meant the first time and what it means now. [Zacharek, final two paragraphs](https://web.archive.org/web/20191015062158/https://www.salon.com/2001/12/25/kate_leopold/) · [Kamiya, paragraphs 21–23](https://web.archive.org/web/20120705121301/https://www.salon.com/2007/12/18/childhood_end/)
2. **Keep the moment the writer's knowledge changes.** In Leonard's energy column, a phone call changes what a House vote appears to mean for a program's future. If an editor led with the resulting judgment and trimmed the call as process detail, readers could miss both who supplied the correction and how tentative it was. Manjoo's account of video-game research also needs its sequence. Two adjacent paragraphs open with the same conjunction. The first asks what the evidence can establish. The next introduces scientists who question how harm was measured. A second “but” earns its place when the reader has reached a different objection. [Leonard, paragraphs 6–8](https://web.archive.org/web/20121117143137/https://www.salon.com/2005/12/16/algae_2/) · [Manjoo, paragraphs 5–7](https://web.archive.org/web/20141023183943/https://www.salon.com/2006/11/11/bully_3/)
3. **Read what the ending actually changes.** Kamiya states what he had misunderstood about watching his children grow up, then turns to address his daughter. A rule to cut explicit statements of theme could take out the change of mind. Havrilesky supplies a counterexample to automatic paragraph joining. After a long comic interrogation in a television column, the narrator answers her own question in an isolated line. The break creates the wait for an answer. In an essay with a run of short paragraphs, test whether a particular line needs that wait. [Kamiya, closing paragraphs](https://web.archive.org/web/20120705121301/https://www.salon.com/2007/12/18/childhood_end/) · [Havrilesky, paragraphs 33–34](https://web.archive.org/web/20191120125313/https://www.salon.com/2005/12/04/i_like_93/)

## How to interpret the counts

The starting set was the earlier library's 24 development pieces. We added two articles each from Rebecca Traister, Farhad Manjoo, Gary Kamiya and Joan Walsh. For each author we queried a single year in the Explorer, took the first two eligible entries returned from the first ten, and inspected archived copies. Eligibility required a single matching byline, available text, no partner outlet in the index, and a pre-2022 capture. The new pieces broaden the close reading. They are a convenience sample. All twelve articles reserved for the earlier evaluation stayed outside this study.

The table counts 18 articles with identifiable comparable spans. Fourteen others supplied context or mixed formats. For example, a year-in-video roundup puts category labels and clip titles in ordinary paragraph tags, and an offshoring column embeds a word-substitution table. Counting either layout as prose rhythm would distort the comparison. Advice totals include the columnist's answer after the salutation and before the footer. Interview totals start with the first conversational turn and include questions and replies. Review and essay totals include dialogue or quotations embedded in their prose.

For each capture, we identified the article container and counted its nonempty innermost HTML paragraph elements after excluding navigation, promotional sidebars, headers and footers. The inventory gives its selector, capture hash, one-based inclusive paragraph span and per-article results. Counts use alphanumeric word tokens, including internally hyphenated words and contractions. Medians pool the paragraphs within each row. We checked both eight- and twelve-word cutoffs. The short closing sentences are a lower bound from inspecting candidate sentence endings in the reviews. Their paragraph locations are recorded in the inventory. A visually single line depends on screen width. Neither cutoff defines a good paragraph.

The reviews come from only two critics, and the four advice replies come from a single columnist. Genre, author and editorial practice overlap. The figures describe this sample and supply counterexamples to mechanical editing rules. They cannot identify authorship or predict which version of a new draft Alonso will prefer.
