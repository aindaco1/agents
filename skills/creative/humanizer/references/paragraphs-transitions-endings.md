# Paragraphs, transitions and endings

Use these comparisons when a prose edit sounds choppy or a transition sounds over-explained. Read the sentence before and after the proposed change. The writer's brief and the requirements of the form govern the edit.

**Try moving the break before rewriting either sentence.** In this invented example, “I brought a spare projector. It was the one that broke.” fits in one paragraph. The second sentence turns on the first, and the period supplies a pause. An interviewer's short question and a guest's equally short reply each need their own turn. A comic column can also use a separate paragraph for a single reply after a long mock interrogation. Read the buildup before deciding how much silence the reply needs. [Leonard on the Memphis airport, paragraphs 1–2](https://web.archive.org/web/20191117002246/https://www.salon.com/2005/12/19/memphis_4/) · [Havrilesky, final exchange before the next-week teaser](https://web.archive.org/web/20191120125313/https://www.salon.com/2005/12/04/i_like_93/)

**Check whether repetition does work across a break.** In a review of *Kate & Leopold*, a character's remark about tourists gives the critic a word for an actor who seems out of place in the film. Switching to a synonym would loosen the connection between the scene and the judgment. Leave the word if the reader can hear its meaning change. A repeated claim with the same speaker, evidence and meaning is a better candidate for cutting. [Zacharek, final two paragraphs](https://web.archive.org/web/20191015062158/https://www.salon.com/2001/12/25/kate_leopold/)

**Preserve the point at which the writer learns something.** A columnist worried about a funding cut makes a call and learns why the outcome is still open. The call changes how much confidence the reader should place in the opening. In a report about video games, two successive paragraphs turn on the same conjunction. The first asks what the evidence shows. The next introduces researchers who dispute what it can establish. Preserve the sequence of claim, source and qualification. Smoothing the passage into one authoritative statement would change its meaning. [Leonard, paragraphs 6–8](https://web.archive.org/web/20121117143137/https://www.salon.com/2005/12/16/algae_2/) · [Manjoo, paragraphs 5–7](https://web.archive.org/web/20141023183943/https://www.salon.com/2006/11/11/bully_3/)

**Test the last sentence for what the reader would lose.** In a personal essay about children growing up, the father corrects his own earlier understanding of what paying attention could give him. He then addresses his daughter directly. Cutting either move as a summary would change the ending. In other drafts, the paragraph may already have made its point before the final line explains it again. Read both versions and keep the one that carries the author's intended meaning. [Kamiya, paragraphs 20–23](https://web.archive.org/web/20120705121301/https://www.salon.com/2007/12/18/childhood_end/)

These examples describe edited historical magazine prose. Paragraph length varies with form and speaker. Counts from an archive do not identify authorship or set a target for a new draft.
