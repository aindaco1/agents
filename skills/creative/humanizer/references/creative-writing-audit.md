# Creative writing and screenplay audit

## SCREENPLAY / CREATIVE-WRITING TELLS FROM JOSHUA SCAMMELL

Use this extra pass for screenplays, fiction, artist statements, essays about creative work, and any prose where voice matters more than clean information delivery.

Scammell's core warning: hiding AI fingerprints is not enough. If the draft has no lived struggle with the material, no sentence-level voice, and no sign that the writer thought their way through the work, the text will still feel machine-made even after the obvious tells are removed. Do not make the piece merely smoother. Restore evidence of a mind making choices.

### Surface tells to scan for

- **Inanimate objects "humming."** AI often gives rooms, lights, neon, machines, cities, or abstract atmosphere a vague hum. Replace with a concrete sound, behavior, or visual detail already available in the draft.
- **En-dash / dash theatrics.** Watch for overused en dashes or em dashes acting as automatic drama. Rewrite with periods, commas, or actual sentence movement.
- **Metaphors that almost work but don't.** If the image sounds poetic but collapses under pressure, cut it or ground it in something physically/socially true.
- **"It's not X, not Y, but Z" constructions.** Especially repeated across pages. Replace with one direct sentence or a sharper contrast.
- **Fragment portrait strings.** Character intros like "Tired. Wired. Half-broken." can work once, but AI overuses "Adjective. Adjective. Compound adjective." Make the description do actual observational work.
- **Fragment environment strings.** Settings described as "Nouns. Nouns. A scattering of nouns." often read generated. Prefer one or two precise details that imply the rest.
- **"That lands."** AI scripts often announce the effect of a hard line instead of letting the line affect the room. Show the silence, interruption, avoidance, mistake, or changed behavior.
- **Word-batting dialogue.** Exchanges like: "That's not a." / "No, it's b." / "Which means c?" / "But not d." create the appearance of thought without anyone saying much. Replace with pressure, conflict, subtext, decision, or a concrete ask.
- **Perfect structure with no residue.** A script can hit every beat and still feel dead. Preserve the stains: contradictions, revisions, awkward choices, and the marks of the writer wrestling with the material.

### Process-and-voice audit

Before finalizing a creative humanization pass, ask:

1. **Does the piece sound like a person thinking, or like a machine completing a prompt?** Writing is thinking; if the text outsources the thinking, the edit must expose the author's own choices rather than polish the outsourcing.
2. **Is the draft outcome-obsessed?** AI-shaped creative work often aims at a clean saleable artifact instead of the pleasure and friction of sentences. Make the sentences feel chosen, not generated.
3. **Is the voice present in every sentence?** Voice is not a decorative layer. It lives in syntax, selection, omissions, patience, and what the writer refuses to over-explain.
4. **Did the idea evolve on the page?** Human writing often shows patience: a thought turning, resisting the first obvious formulation, discovering the expression and the content together. Do not flatten that movement into perfect summary.
5. **Is the work too perfect?** In the coming flood of machine-clean scripts and essays, over-polished competence is itself suspicious. A beating heart beats polished perfection.

### Editing rule

For constrained edits, do not invent new scenes, facts, metaphors, or autobiographical detail to solve these problems. If the draft lacks the material needed for real voice, mark the gap or ask for source material. For authorial rewrites, add specificity, friction, side beats, and sentence-level choices only when the user has allowed that scope.
