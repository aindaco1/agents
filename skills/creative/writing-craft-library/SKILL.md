# Writing craft library

Use a few observed techniques to solve a named writing problem. This library
contains original commentary on historical Salon passages, including useful
counterexamples. Publication, age, and a human byline do not certify quality or
make an article a factual authority for a new draft.

Read `references/index.md`, select at most two cards that fit the genre and
problem, and read those cards. The index links to the original article and exact
Wayback capture through `references/sources.json`. The package contains analysis
and source metadata, not an anthology of copied articles. Load only the material
needed; do not put the whole library into every writing task.

1. Establish the task's preservation contract, intended reader, and genre. The
   user's source and voice samples outrank outside exemplars. For strict editing,
   use `constrained-humanization-editing`; additions are still prohibited.
2. Before editing, identify what is already working. A long sentence, deliberate
   repetition, aside, fragment, plain explanation, list, or explicit conclusion
   is not a defect by itself. Give every proposed edit a reader-facing reason.
3. Name one actual problem and choose a relevant card. If there is no problem,
   return the passage unchanged. Do not manufacture an improvement to prove that
   the library was used.
4. Transfer the function of the technique. Do not copy distinctive language,
   duplicate an article's structure, imitate a writer's verbal tics, or import
   its incidents, opinions, or reported facts. Never turn a source's speculation
   into fact. Interview replies belong to the speaker; advice letters belong to
   their writers, not to the columnist.
5. Use only source-supported material. If the move needs a scene, quote, object,
   motive, or evidence the draft lacks, omit it or identify the missing input.
   An attractive invented detail is still an invented detail. In fiction, new
   material requires a brief that authorizes invention.
6. Compare before and after for claims, stance, scope, uncertainty, protected
   quotations, and formatting. Restore any deliberate effect lost in revision.
   Follow the owning skill's pass limit and output contract. Do not add a craft
   explanation when the user requested only revised text.

For criticism and creative nonfiction, cards can guide observation, argument,
rhythm and endings. For interviews, preserve who said what and the status of
quoted speech. For technical, academic, legal, medical, or operational prose,
prioritize precision and appropriate uncertainty; a magazine's wit or narrative
shape may be irrelevant. These cards are not professional advice or a substitute
for verification. For ghostwriting, derive the voice from the principal's own
material; outside references only widen the available techniques.

The library does not classify authorship or optimize detector scores. Follow
`humanizer` for detector-related requests. A dated archive is a useful historical
reference, not proof that a model has never seen the text.

For a new reference, inspect the actual article boundary, byline, publication
date and capture timestamp; keep them separate. Do not infer missing passages
from snippets. Exclude navigation, ads, reader comments, author biographies and
other articles. Record partial access, extraction changes, syndicated origins,
and uncertainty. Use authorized copies and minimal quotation.
