# Finding comparable buyers and campaigns

Start with the decision in the brief. “Would a school organizer request a temporary hold?” calls for different sources from “Would an exhibitor buy screening rights?” Record the buying unit, the action and its cost or commitment. Add channel, geography, price range and purchase stage when known. If the user only has an idea, state your working audience and start searching.

## Find and screen examples

1. **Search in the buyer's language.** Look for the work they were trying to accomplish and verbs like *shortlisted*, *switched*, *renewed*, *rejected*, *sponsored* or *deferred*. Try searches such as `"movie theater" "field trip" "PTA" minutes` or, for software, `"[product]" "switched from" "[alternative]"`. Search for someone who stayed with the incumbent or abandoned the purchase. Buyer-authored posts, procurement decisions, original meeting records and first-person reviews can reveal reasons missing from seller case studies.
2. **Look up the actual offer separately.** Search the original seller's pricing, booking instructions, campaign, or archived terms for the period in question. A seller can establish its deposit, trial or cancellation terms. A buyer who encountered those terms can establish how they affected one decision. Favor independent evidence from both sides. Trace syndicated stories and repeated testimonials back to one origin.
3. **Look for outcomes if the question needs them.** Search for the action plus `randomized`, `control`, `conversion` or `replication data`. For a benchmark, inspect treatment and control, exposure unit, eligible denominator, exact event, observation window and missing data. Record which materials are available to reproduce the stimulus. A conversion figure without an eligible denominator, an ad-library listing, and a customer quote each support different claims. If no matching result exists, report that gap. Reading published answers makes them known to the researcher; a later replay requires an independent holdout for an accuracy claim.
4. **Open each original and record what transfers.** For each candidate provide the buyer and decision, one supported observation, URL and page/section, date, and the largest mismatch with the user's setting. Label the source as buyer account, policy, seller offer or measured comparison. Prefer a small set with distinct evidence to many mentions of the same transaction. Show a close match, an adjacent case that challenges the explanation, and any missing buyer or endpoint where available. If sources disagree, preserve the disagreement. If all the evidence comes from sellers, provide offer ideas and keep buyer behavior unknown.
5. **Deliver at the level requested.** For discovery alone, return the table plus the most useful next question. For a panel, use one case per distinct buying unit or explicitly hypothetical policy setting. Enter `basis: public-analogue`, a direct `source_url`, and a dated locator in `source`. Put only decision-relevant facts and visible unknowns in `evidence`. Never combine another organization's purchasing policy with an actual buyer's story and attribute the result to that buyer. An invented budget, persona, or composite is illustrative. Describe analogue counts as coverage of the chosen situations. Estimates of frequency or conversion require their own sampling and outcome evidence.

These findings suggest asking which step requires spending approval: an inquiry, a free hold, or a binding booking. The [runnable study](../examples/public-school-screenings.json) encodes the two buyer-side sources separately. Its offer prices, 48-hour holds and outreach are hypothetical. None of the four sources measures a change in cinema bookings caused by moving the deposit.

**A different search for a marketing outcome.** Hillstrom's public retail email challenge describes random assignment of 64,000 recent customers to two merchandise campaigns and a no-email control. It records site visits and purchases over two weeks. This is a closer comparison for retention email than for cold outbound or cinema reservations. The released fields describe merchandise categories and do not include the full email creative. The public dataset can exercise the analysis workflow; its numbers belong to that experiment. [Original study and field definitions](https://blog.minethatdata.com/2008/03/minethatdata-e-mail-analytics-and-data.html?m=0) · [Open-source dataset loader](https://www.uplift-modeling.com/en/latest/api/datasets/fetch_hillstrom.html)

## Platforms supported by optional contributions

For a two-sided service, write down who decides to adopt it, who makes the purchase and who receives each amount. Use the owner's actual terms for subscriptions, commissions, provider accounts, processing, storage and refunds. Preserve those terms across arms unless one of them is the proposed change. An optional platform tip, a payment to a creator and a reimbursement of processing costs can each appear at checkout and serve different purposes.

Use adoption cases for the seller panel. For the payer panel, specify whether the question is about completing a purchase, leaving a positive tip, or selecting a particular amount. The binary instrument can answer one of these per study. A zero tip can accompany a completed sale. If the buyer's next decision remains unknown, keep that result. A prior gift at a charity checkout alone cannot establish willingness to tip for a product.

Use transactions created after the option was verifiably offered. Distinguish paid transactions from promises and include zero tips in the eligible denominator. Check whether the suggested amount changed, how nonstandard checkouts were configured, and whether several line items share one payment. Measure tip participation among paid orders, actual tip dollars per paid order, and actual tip dollars per checkout started. A panel of selected cases supplies none of those population rates. For a financial scenario, use explicit prices, assumed behavior and operating costs, with measured benchmarks labeled by source and setting.

For evidence on defaults, Altmann and colleagues randomized the suggested platform contribution on Betterplace separately from the main charitable donation. Participants could choose zero. This provides an example of measuring both opting out and dollars raised. Once the result informs the design, it serves as a benchmark for interpretation. Validation requires another outcome held out from design and inference. [Original study, section III.C](https://curis.ku.dk/ws/files/235962944/rest_a_00774.pdf)
